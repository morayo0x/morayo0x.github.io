Dear sir,

A small observation about method, offered without much confidence.

I do my thinking while walking, and I have stopped treating this as a quirk. It
is too reliable. The problems that will not move at a desk move after forty
minutes on the road to Yaba, and they move in a particular way — not by my
solving them, but by their turning slightly, so that the part I had been staring
at is no longer the part facing me.

I have wondered why, and I have two candidate explanations which I cannot choose
between.

The first is mechanical and unglamorous: walking occupies exactly enough of the
attention to stop the anxious part of the mind from supervising, and the
supervision was the obstruction all along.

The second is that a problem held still is a problem seen from one position, and
that the mind borrows its sense of _moving around_ a thing from the body
actually moving around things. On this account the walking is not a distraction
from the thought; it is the thought's only available metaphor for changing
position, and it has to be enacted to be used.

I prefer the second. I notice that I prefer it because it is more interesting,
which is not a reason, and that the first explains the evidence just as well
with fewer commitments.

Grothendieck said the sea does not advance by breaking but by rising. I have
always read that as being about patience. Lately I read it as being about not
watching.

Yours sincerely, Morayo
